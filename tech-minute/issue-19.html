WHY THE “BOSS” IS EMAILING FROM GMAIL
It arrives looking ordinary. The name on the email is someone you know: your supervisor, a program director, a VP. The note is short and friendly. “Are you at your desk?” “I need a quick favor.” “Can you pick up a few gift cards for a family in need? I'll pay you back today.”

Look closely at the sender and the address isn't a cfsny.org address at all. It's a Gmail account, or something that only looks like ours. The scammer never broke into anyone's mailbox. They found a leader's name and title on our website or LinkedIn, opened a free email account, and typed that name in as the display name. On a phone, the name is all you see. That's the whole trick, and it works often enough that it's now the most common scam aimed at nonprofits like ours.
THREE THINGS TO CHECK BEFORE YOU ACT
Any time an email from a CFS leader asks you to do something unusual, run this check. It takes less time than reading this sentence:
  1. The address, not the name. Tap or hover on the sender's name to see the real email address. CFS leaders email you from @cfsny.org. Gmail, Yahoo, Outlook.com, or a close lookalike (an extra letter, a dash, a .net) is the giveaway.
  2. The ask. Gift cards, a wire or bank change, a personal cell number to text, “keep this between us,” “I'm in a meeting and can't talk.” No one at CFS will ever ask you to buy gift cards or move money by email. That is simply not how we do things.
  3. Verify another way. Don't reply to the email. Send the real person a Teams message or call them at the number you already have. Thirty seconds settles it, and nobody will ever mind being asked.
If it's fake, click Report at the top of the message in Outlook and choose Report phishing, or forward it to HelpDesk@cfsny.org. We block the sender for everyone. And if you've already replied or bought the cards, tell the Help Desk right away. Nobody gets in trouble for being targeted. Speed is what matters.
Pro tip: Your phone hides the clue. The Outlook mobile app shows the sender's name in big letters and the address only after you tap that name. If an unusual request from a CFS leader lands on your phone, tap the name before you do anything else, or wait until you're back at a computer. A real boss can wait ten minutes. A scammer can't.
Try this with Charlie: Ask, “I got an email that looks like it's from my supervisor, but it came from a Gmail address and asks me to buy gift cards. What should I do?” Then share this issue with someone on your team who mostly reads email on their phone.
