CONFIDENT AND WRONG LOOK THE SAME
AI tools write in complete, confident sentences whether they're right or not. That's the trap. A wrong answer from Copilot or Charlie doesn't come with a warning label; it reads exactly like a right one, with the same polish and the same certainty. Sometimes the tool fills a gap with something that merely sounds plausible: a policy number that doesn't exist, a date that's close but off, a name attached to the wrong role.

None of that makes the tools bad. It makes them fast first drafts. Copilot and Charlie are at their best when they save you the blank page and you supply the last five percent: the check.
WHICH ANSWERS TO DOUBLE CHECK
Not every answer needs a second look. A summary of an email thread or a cleaner version of your own paragraph, you can judge just by reading it. Three kinds of output always get verified before they leave your hands:
  1. Facts you'll act on. Names, dates, dollar amounts, regulation citations, policy numbers. If the AI says a policy requires something, open that policy and look.
  2. Anything that goes into a record. Progress notes, incident reports, plans, or a message to a family. AI can help you draft it, but you own every word once it's saved or sent.
  3. Answers about how CFS does things. Charlie answers from our policies and knowledge base and shows you the article it used. Copilot draws on your files and the web. Either way, if no source is shown, ask for one, and if it can't give you one, treat the answer as a guess.
And the fastest check of all: does it sound like CFS? If a draft describes a program we don't run, a form we don't use, or a step your team has never taken, trust that instinct and look it up.
Pro tip: Charlie lists the knowledge base article or policy it used at the end of its answer. Click it. Thirty seconds reading the actual policy beats a confident summary of it, and it's the same habit we ask for with email: go to the source, not the description of the source.
Try this with Charlie: Ask it any policy question, then follow up with “Which document did you use for that answer?” and open what it points to.
