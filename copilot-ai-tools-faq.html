Getting Started

I don’t see the Copilot icon in Teams. How do I find it and keep it there?

This was the single biggest thing holding people back at an in person training in September, so we slowed down on it at the October 13 session. Here is the fix.

  • In Teams, look at the left rail where Chat and Calendar live. If Copilot is not there, click the three dots (More) at the bottom of that rail.
  • Type Copilot in the search box. When it appears, click the three dots next to it and choose Pin.
  • Copilot now stays on your left rail every time you open Teams.

If you do not see the left rail at all, only the meeting you are in, your Teams window is probably in a compact or pop out view. Maximize the window, or close the meeting pop out, and the rail comes back. If it still does not, email the Help Desk and we will fix it with you on a quick screen share.

Another Way In

Copilot Chat also works in a browser. Go to m365.cloud.microsoft/chat, sign in with your @cfsny.org account, and check for the green shield. It is the same protected Copilot you see in Teams.

I opened Copilot in Teams but I don’t see the Work and Web tabs. Is something wrong?

Nothing is wrong. Microsoft is replacing the Work and Web tabs, and the change reaches accounts at different times, which is why one person’s Copilot looked different from the one on screen during training. If your Copilot shows a Work IQ chip at the top left with an Auto selector beside it, you have the new interface. Work IQ means Copilot is grounded in your CFS data, and Auto lets it decide when a general question should also check the web. If you still see Work and Web tabs, you have the older interface, and it works the same way.

Either way, the check that matters is the green shield at the top right (see Data Protection below). If you see the shield, you are inside the CFS tenant and protected. If you see neither the shield nor any sign of Work IQ or Work and Web, email the Help Desk so we can confirm your setup.

Nothing breaks if you don’t start in Teams. We recommend Teams because it is a one stop shop: Chat, Calendar, OneDrive, and the Copilot icon all sit on the left rail, so one habit gets you to everything. If you prefer to open Copilot from Outlook, from the Microsoft 365 app, from a browser, or from Edge with your CFS account, the same protections apply as long as you see the green shield (see Data Protection below).

Which Copilot do I have? I keep hearing about different versions.

Think of it as three keys to the same building. Every CFS staff member has at least two of them.

  • Copilot Chat. Everyone has this. Open it from the Copilot icon in Teams. It answers questions, drafts and rewrites text, summarizes documents you upload, and in Work mode it can look at your own email, calendar, and files.
  • Microsoft 365 Copilot (the full license). A smaller group of staff have this. It puts Copilot inside Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook, and Teams meetings, so it can build a presentation from a Word document, add a calculated column in Excel, or give you a meeting recap.
  • Charlie. Everyone has this too. Charlie is the CFS agent on the intranet home page. It only knows our policies, procedures, guides, and training materials, and it cites them when it answers.

The Quick Reference Card on the IT Help & Resources page shows which features belong to which key.

I got lost during the hands on exercise. What exactly were we supposed to do?

Here it is step by step, and you can do it right now.

  • Open Teams and click the Copilot icon on the left rail (it sits with Chat, Calendar, and OneDrive).
  • Type Turn these notes into an agenda. Then paste the block of meeting notes from the session chat underneath it and press Enter. Copilot builds what it thinks an agenda is.
  • Start a new prompt with the same notes, but change only the instruction: Turn these notes into a 30 minute agenda. Put the items in a sensible order and give each one a time allocation. Try 15 minutes too and watch it tighten up.

Compare the results. The notes never changed; only the way you asked changed, and you got a completely different answer. That is the one skill the whole series is built on. Everything else we showed is that skill pointed at your inbox, your meetings, and your files.

Two things people noticed during the exercise. First, the order does not matter: Anthony likes to give the instruction first and the notes second, Jim does it the other way around, and both work. Second, your result will not match your neighbor’s even with identical notes and an identical prompt. Copilot words things a little differently every time. That is normal, not a mistake.

What makes a good prompt? Mine came out nothing like Anthony’s.

A prompt has four parts: Goal (what you want), Context (who it is for and why), Source (what Copilot should work from), and Format (bullets, a table, a 30 minute agenda, a short email). “Write an email about the Copilot training” is missing three of the four, so Copilot has to guess. Try it yourself and notice what Copilot does not know: the date, the time, who the email is for, and where the join link is. It is not a mind reader. Give it the same detail you would give a brand new coworker on their first day, because that is about what it knows about your situation.

One more trick from the October session: tell Copilot who to be. “Act as an HR coordinator and write this update for program directors” shifts the vocabulary, the tone, and what it pays attention to. The same request as a finance manager or an IT professional comes back differently.

You never have to start with a blank box. The Copilot Prompt Library on the IT Help & Resources page has ready made prompts organized by department, all built on this four part pattern. If nothing fits, scroll to the bottom of the library, describe what you need in plain English, click Generate, and it writes a prompt you can copy straight into Copilot.

Does Copilot learn how I like things over time?

Yes, within a conversation and, to a degree, across them. Every staff member’s Copilot is individual to them. The way you talk to it, the corrections you make, and the tone you ask for all shape what you get back. One colleague’s Copilot has learned to close out the day with a joke; Jim’s does not. Spend a little time with yours and it starts to sound like your assistant rather than a generic one.

Two practical tips from Anthony’s demo. If you tell Copilot “keep this chat warm and professional,” that instruction sticks for that chat only. And when you move to a new topic, click New chat in the corner rather than piling everything into one long conversation. A fresh chat gives you a clean slate, and your earlier chats stay in the list if you need them.

Does it help to be polite to Copilot?

It does not hurt, and it tends to produce warmer, more natural drafts. Jim starts most prompts with “Good morning” and ends with “please” and “thank you” out of habit (and, as he says, so he knows which side he will be on when the robots arrive). What matters more than manners is being specific about what you want and what it should look like.

Data Protection & Where the Line Is

What exactly has to stay out of any AI tool?

Jim put on his HIPAA Security Officer hat for this one so there is no doubt. The following stays out of Copilot, out of Charlie, out of ChatGPT at home, out of everything, unless a CFS policy specifically allows it under controlled conditions:

  • Client names together with their information, and anything that counts as PHI (protected health information) or PII (personally identifiable information)
  • Social Security numbers and dates of birth
  • Credit card and bank account numbers
  • Educational records

This applies at home as much as at work. Do not hand your bank statements to ChatGPT to build a budget; give it made up numbers and plug in the real ones yourself afterward. The same habit works at CFS: Copilot writes just as well about “J.R.” or a fictional name as it does about a real one, and you can put the real details back in your own document.

If you are unsure whether a specific task is allowed, ask Charlie first. Charlie knows the AI Acceptable Use policy and will tell you where the line is before you paste anything.

The Hesitation Rule

If you feel even a small pull of hesitation before you paste something, that hesitation is your answer. Stop, reread what you are about to enter, and ask yourself why you paused.

We get long referral packets with dates of birth, medical and behavioral information. Can I put one into Copilot to summarize it?

Yes, with two conditions: you are in Work mode and you see the green shield that says your data is protected by CFS. Under those conditions your Copilot is your assistant sitting across the desk from you. It can only see what your CFS account already sees, your prompts and uploads stay inside our Microsoft 365 environment, and they are not used to train Microsoft’s models. Uploading a 30 page packet and asking for the five things you need is exactly what it is for.

A good habit from the session: if you feel yourself hesitate before pasting something, that hesitation is the answer. Stop, take five seconds, confirm you are in Work mode with the shield showing, and then proceed.

The Rule Is About the Door, Not the Document

The same packet must never go into the Web tab of Copilot, into ChatGPT, Gemini, Grok, or any other public AI tool. Once it leaves our walls, CFS can no longer protect it.

What is the difference between the Work tab and the Web tab in Copilot Chat?

Work looks inside the agency: your OneDrive and SharePoint files, your Outlook mail and calendar, your Teams meetings. Web searches the public internet, outside our protected environment. It is fine for “who won last night’s game” or a general question. Use Work for anything involving CFS information, and if you switch to Web, do not upload or paste organizational content while you are there. Jim lives in Work and rarely checks the toggle, which is a good habit to build.

If your Copilot shows Work IQ and Auto instead of two tabs, the same idea applies: Work IQ is the protected, inside the agency mode, and you are in it whenever the green shield is showing. See I Don’t See Work and Web above.

Can I use ChatGPT, Gemini, or another AI tool for CFS work?

No. For CFS work, use only Copilot inside our Microsoft 365 environment, or Charlie on the intranet. This is CFS policy (IT-AI-001, AI Acceptable Use), and the reason is simple: those tools run outside our protective boundaries, so once agency or resident information goes in, we cannot get it back or control where it goes. ChatGPT and similar sites are blocked on the CFS network for that reason, whether you are on CFS Corp, CFS Guest, or a virtual desktop.

Nobody is judging what you use at home. They are not bad tools. But what you do at home stays at home: do not bring CFS content to them, and even for personal use keep your own sensitive information out (see What Must Stay Out above).

I need to draft an incident report. Can Copilot help, and what should I keep out of the prompt?

The narrative of an incident report has to be in your own words; that is what the policy requires and what an auditor expects. So the wrong approach is to paste the whole draft, names and all, into any AI tool and say “fix the grammar and flow.” Think about what goes into one of these reports: a date, a location, what happened to a specific person. That is exactly the information that must stay out.

What Copilot can do is help with structure and clarity. Ask it a general question such as “What makes an incident report clear and complete?” and use the answer to tighten your own writing. If you do paste a passage for polishing, stay in Work mode, strip every identifying detail first (initials or a fictional name, no dates of birth, nothing the sentence does not need), and put the real details back in your own document afterward.

If you are unsure what is allowed, ask Charlie: “Can I use Copilot to help write an incident report?” Charlie answers from the CFS policy. The result: the report stays yours, the client’s information stays where it belongs, and you still get help making it readable.

Back to top

When should I not use AI at all?

Any conversation that requires your judgment and your own voice. A hard conversation with a family, a board member, an employee, or a colleague deserves your words, not a draft you reviewed. If Jim has something serious to say to Peter, he wants Peter to know it came from him, from the heart, not from an AI. Those moments call for dialogue, listening, and your presence.

There is a middle ground. If you have written something difficult yourself and want a second set of eyes, you can ask Copilot to soften the tone or check the flow. The difference is that it starts with your words and your thinking. What you should not do is ask Copilot to originate the message for you. Copilot is for the work around those moments: the summary, the follow up notes, the agenda, the first draft of a routine update.

Getting Good Results

Have you ever had to argue with Copilot when it gives you the wrong thing?

All the time, and that is not a sign you are doing it wrong. Think of it as a very smart new intern on day one. When the result is not right, tell it plainly what is wrong and how you want it instead: “That’s not it. I want three bullets, no introduction, and use the template from last month.” Keep refining. If a conversation goes sideways, start a fresh one. Jim has used Copilot daily for over a year and still corrects it regularly; that back and forth is you teaching it how you communicate. And sometimes, as Jim admits, the problem was that he did not ask it correctly in the first place.

Back to top

How much should I trust what Copilot gives me?

Treat it as a first draft, never a final decision. Copilot is confident even when it is wrong, and the confidence does not come with accuracy. Jim has two recent examples. He asked Copilot to build a spreadsheet from data he supplied; it came back clean and professional, and a 30 second read showed it had quietly added plausible, fictional data to fill gaps. Another time it drafted a summary that cited a CFS policy number that does not exist. Neither was a malicious lie. Copilot filled a gap with something that sounded right. The term for that is a hallucination; Jim calls it a mistake.

Before anything goes out under your name, check three things:

  • Facts and numbers. Anything that matters, check against the source.
  • Tone. Does it sound like you, or like a robot? If Peter sends Jim a very formal email, Jim knows it is not Peter talking.
  • Say it out loud. If you would say that exact sentence to a family member, a colleague, or an auditor, send it. If you hesitate, push back: “Try again, make it sound like this.”

Reading the draft is not distrusting the tool. Reading the draft is most of the job.

Trust, but Verify

You are responsible for what you send. Copilot writes the first version; you write the final.

Copilot took 20 or 30 seconds to answer during the demo. Is it supposed to be that slow?

Yes, and it feels longer than it is. Watching Copilot search a month of email, read a 12 page document, or build a Word file is a little like watching water boil. But compare it to the alternative: the email search Jim demonstrated would have taken him an hour by hand and Copilot finished in under a minute. Charlie can take a few extra seconds because it is searching the entire CFS policy library before it answers. If something is still spinning after a minute or two, start a new chat and try again; if it keeps happening, let the Help Desk know.

Can Copilot give me the result as a Word document or a PowerPoint instead of text in the chat?

Yes. After Copilot gives you a summary, a letter, or a set of bullets, just type Export this to Word (or to PowerPoint). Copilot builds the file and gives you a link to open it, with no need to open Word first. Peter demonstrated this on a document he had never opened: five bullet summary, export to Word, done in under a minute. You can also tell Copilot the format up front, for example “one page, under 300 words, export to Word” or “under 10 slides, export to PowerPoint.” Open the file afterward to edit further, or save and send it as is once you have read it.

Back to top

Can Copilot work with a document I scanned on the copier?

Yes. A scanned document becomes a PDF, and Copilot can read PDFs and Word documents alike. Upload it, or point Copilot at it in OneDrive by typing a forward slash and picking the file, then ask for what you need: “Read these 30 pages and give me the five most important points.” Two things to remember: be specific about what you want pulled out, or you will get five bullets about the wrong thing, and check the highlights against the original before you rely on them.

Email, Meetings & Files

What is the morning briefing in Outlook, and why don’t I see it?

New Outlook opens with a Copilot card at the top of your inbox that summarizes what came in overnight, what is waiting on a reply from you, and what is on your calendar today. It is not an email from a person; it sits above your messages and is labeled as your morning briefing. No license is needed; it works for everyone with Copilot Chat. And it is a conversation: ask “What needs a decision from me today?” or “Draft a reply to the most urgent one and keep it warm and short,” then read and adjust before you send.

Several people at the September 22 session did not see it, and the reason was timing. The briefing appears the first time you open Outlook each day. If the first thing you do is start reading and replying, Outlook assumes you do not need it and the card goes away, and if you have already handled email on your phone before you sit down, you have used up your morning. So tomorrow, before you answer a single message, open Outlook on your computer and look at the top of your inbox. Use the new Outlook desktop app rather than the browser or your phone. If you do that and still do not see it, email the Help Desk and we will look at your setup.

Try This Tomorrow

Open Outlook, click the briefing, and type: Catch me up on the emails in my inbox. Then ask it one follow up question. Everyone’s briefing is different because everyone’s inbox is different.

When Jim asked Copilot in Teams to find a month of emails, was it searching Teams messages or his actual email?

His actual Outlook email. This surprised a few people. The Copilot window in Teams is not limited to Teams; in Work mode it is a window onto your whole Microsoft 365 account, so it can read your Outlook mail and calendar, your OneDrive and SharePoint files, and your Teams meetings. Jim never opened Outlook. He typed “Summarize the email thread about the holiday coverage schedule and tell me who is waiting on me,” and Copilot found a month of back and forth with Peter, Estefani, and Anthony, summarized it, listed the next actions, and then drafted a reply on request. A task that would have taken an hour of searching took about two minutes.

Note that searching your mailbox this way is a licensed feature. Copilot Chat users can get the same benefit by pasting an email thread into the chat and asking the same questions.

I was out for a week. Can Copilot catch me up?

Yes. When Peter polled the room, people said catching up after two days out takes anywhere from an hour to a full day. In Copilot Chat (Work mode) ask something like “Summarize what I missed since Thursday, flag anything that needs my action, and tell me who is waiting on a reply from me.” It takes 20 seconds to type and flags the three messages that matter out of 60. Then read the messages it flags yourself. Copilot points; you decide. All four catch up prompts from the slide are in the Prompt Library word for word.

How do I get Copilot to record and summarize my meetings?

This is the question Peter hears at every training, and the answer is that Copilot does not record anything on its own. The organizer does. When your meeting starts, click More, then Record and transcribe, and let people know you are recording. You can also add the Facilitator to the meeting and it takes live AI notes for everyone.

No transcript means no recap, so if you organize meetings, make turning it on at the start a habit. Afterward, open the meeting in your Teams calendar or chat and look for the Recap tab. Future you will thank you.

Does everyone who attends a Teams meeting get the recording and notes?

It depends on how the meeting was set up. If the meeting was scheduled inside a Teams channel, everyone in that channel gets a post with the recording, transcript, and notes automatically. If it was a regular meeting (invitees who are not in a shared channel, or people outside the agency), the organizer decides after the meeting whether to share the recording with everyone, with certain people, or with no one. Recordings and transcripts are stored in the channel’s SharePoint site or the organizer’s OneDrive.

What can licensed users do in Teams meetings?

With a full license, meeting recap generates notes, decisions, and action items within minutes of a meeting ending, and you can interrogate it: “What was decided about the October schedule?” “What was assigned to me?” “Was my name mentioned?” If you joined 20 minutes late, ask it to catch you up on the part you missed. Peter calls it the best thing in the license. Remember that the recap only exists if transcription was on (see above).

One more licensed trick: scheduling. Ask Copilot to “find 30 minutes next week with Peter, Anthony, and Estefani” and it checks everyone’s calendar and drafts the invite. No more reply all asking who is free Thursday.

Without a license, the recording and transcript still work exactly the way we taught in Series 2, and you can paste a transcript into Copilot Chat and ask it the same questions.

Copilot says it cannot find a file I know I have. Why?

Copilot can only see files that live in OneDrive or SharePoint. If a document is sitting on your Desktop, in your local Documents folder, on the C: drive, or still on the H: drive, Copilot will not find it when it searches, and the forward slash file picker will not show it. Move it into OneDrive and it appears.

There is a second reason to do this that has nothing to do with Copilot: files outside OneDrive are not backed up. If that laptop fails, the document you spent three weeks on is gone. Jim has lived through that and does not want you to. If you still have files outside OneDrive, email the Help Desk. We will help you move everything and set up a folder structure that makes sense to you.

Can Copilot see other people’s files, or can they see mine?

No. Copilot cannot open anything you cannot open. It uses the same OneDrive, the same SharePoint, and the same permissions you learned about in Series 1. Peter cannot see Jim’s files, Jim cannot see Anthony’s, and the only way any of them see each other’s documents is when a file or folder has been shared for collaboration. Copilot is a new way in to your own content; it is not new access to anyone else’s.

Which features need the full Microsoft 365 Copilot license?

Anything inside a Microsoft 365 app or across your whole account: Copilot in Word (draft or rewrite a document), Excel (add a calculated column, find which unit had the most overtime, explain what a sheet is showing you), PowerPoint (build a deck from a Word document), Outlook’s inbox prioritization (importance scores and one line summaries, turned on under Settings > Copilot, and the feature licensed colleagues mention most), searching your mailbox and files directly from the chat, meeting recap, and scheduling across calendars.

Copilot Chat, the morning briefing, exporting a chat result to Word or PowerPoint, and Charlie are available to everyone. The license’s advantage is mostly that it works on the file or mailbox directly, with no copying and pasting.

Charlie & Agents

What is the difference between Charlie and Copilot?

The simplest way to put it: anything about CFS, how we do things, what a policy says, who to contact, how many days off you get, a question about your 401k, that is Charlie. Anything about your work, your email, your meetings, your documents, that is Copilot.

An agent is a Copilot with a job description and a restricted knowledge base. Charlie is CFS’s agent: it only knows the policies, procedures, guides, and training materials we have given it, and it cites those documents, with links, when it answers. Anthony asked both tools “How do I share a SharePoint file with specific people?” during the demo. Copilot gave good general steps. Charlie gave the same steps plus the CFS policy behind them and the Help Desk address if you get stuck. Charlie takes a few seconds longer because it is searching our whole policy library first.

Charlie lives on the right side of the intranet home page, and because the intranet is ours, Microsoft never moves him. The header of the chat window tells you that you are talking to Charlie, so if the two ever look alike, check the top of the window.

Rule of Thumb

Charlie is the front desk. Copilot is your desk.

Someone in my department wants to build an HR bot. Should we?

You already have one. At an in person Copilot training in September, several staff tried to build an HR bot and the answers it gave about days off and 401k questions were coming back wrong. That is exactly the job Charlie was built for. Charlie’s knowledge base includes the HR policies, so ask him first. If Charlie gives you an answer that does not match what HR tells you, email the Help Desk: it may mean a policy changed and we need to update his knowledge base, which we do with HR. Please do not build a parallel bot; two sources that disagree are worse than one.

Why does Charlie sometimes not know something?

Because it was not given the document. If a new handbook or procedure has not been added to Charlie’s knowledge base, Charlie cannot use it. If you ask Charlie something it should know and it comes up empty or wrong, email the Help Desk; adding or refreshing a document is quick, and your report is how we keep Charlie accurate.
